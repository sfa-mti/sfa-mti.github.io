Smart Finance Advisor

SFA is a personal finance application for people who use several bank accounts and want to manage their finances more effectively. This page documents our team, the project and our progress during the course.

03

Problem

When spending is spread across several accounts, checking each banking app does not always make it easy to see the total or compare it with a monthly budget.

01

Spending across accounts

Total spending, recurring payments and budget progress can be difficult to review together.

02

Manual tracking

Collecting and categorizing data takes time before users can evaluate their spending.

03

Data privacy

Users need to understand what financial information they share and how it will be processed.

04

Solution and value proposition

We plan to bring transactions from supported bank accounts into SFA, group expenses by category and compare them with the user’s budget. Recommendations will explain which spending patterns may be worth reviewing.

The aim is to reduce the work needed to review spending across accounts and decide where to adjust a budget.
Project vision

Core functionality

  • Personal finance dashboard
  • Income and expense tracking
  • Expense categorization and spending analysis
  • Budget creation and monitoring
  • Basic financial insights and recommendations
Planned development

Future enhancements

  • Consent-based, read-only Open Banking integration
  • Automatic recurring-transaction detection
  • Personalized budget alerts and recommendations
  • AI-generated financial summaries
  • Improved data visualization and user experience

We have not selected an Open Banking provider yet. We need to check bank coverage and costs, explain what data is shared, and implement access revocation and deletion. We also want to limit the information sent to external AI services.

05

Customer segments

Primary segment

People in Romania who actively use at least two bank accounts.

They want to manage their spending without repeatedly consolidating their finances by hand.

Initial recruitment

We plan to start with students who have an income and people in their first years of work, using university and professional contacts to find interview participants.

We are starting with individual budgets. We may explore household budgeting later if interviews show a need for it.

07

Proposed competitive advantage

We want users to be able to check why SFA makes a recommendation, choose whether to follow it and review the result later.

  1. Identify

    Find a spending change or a budget that needs attention.

  2. Explain

    Show the period, transactions and calculation behind the suggestion.

  3. Choose

    Let the user correct the data, reject the suggestion or choose an adjustment.

  4. Review

    Compare later spending with the adjustment the user chose.

We need to find out whether this is more useful than the tools people already use. Competitors also offer bank connections and AI features, so those alone are not enough to set SFA apart.

Customer DiscoveryMilestone 2 · 02 November 2026
M2
01

Problem identification & initial solution

Our starting hypothesis was that using several financial accounts makes it harder to understand personal spending.

M1 framed the problem around fragmented information, manual consolidation and the difficulty of turning transaction history into useful decisions. This was a product hypothesis, not an established fact about everyone with several accounts. We used discovery to investigate when people actually need a total, how they obtain it and whether the effort matters to them.

Question guiding discovery: when does an existing method fail to provide a useful answer, or require enough unwanted work that someone would change it?

02

Customer discovery plan & process

Our approach is to study recent behaviour before asking people to evaluate SFA: identify an actual spending question, reconstruct the current workflow, understand its consequences, and only then compare a solution on the same task.

  1. Explore current behaviour. An online questionnaire asks about the previous 30 days, the most recent attempt to calculate spending, tools, time, difficulty and account connections. Open questions also ask what worked well.
  2. Look for different behaviours. Cross-tabulate account count with checking frequency, method, time, difficulty and connection experience. Read free-text answers for both friction and successful alternatives.
  3. Investigate a narrower segment. Document individual interviews and observe the current workflow before showing SFA. Then test the MVP, correct totals and repeat use. These next activities are described below as planned work and are not done yet.

Questionnaire & analysis rules

The questionnaire focuses on what respondents did, rather than whether they like the idea of an AI finance app. It asks about successful workflows as well as difficulties.

Read all 13 questions — original Romanian wording

  2. La câte bănci sau servicii financiare, precum Revolut, ai folosit efectiv conturi personale în ultimele 30 de zile?
  3. Pentru ce folosești conturile respective?
    Poți da unul-două exemple: un cont pentru salariu, altul pentru cumpărături sau economii. Poți menționa banca ori aplicația, fără numere de cont sau sume.
  4. În ultimele 30 de zile, de câte ori ai încercat să afli cât ai cheltuit în total într-o anumită perioadă?
    De exemplu, totalul unei săptămâni sau al unei luni.
  5. Ce te-a făcut să verifici atunci?
    Un exemplu în una-două propoziții este suficient.
  6. Cum ai încercat să afli totalul?
    Bifează metodele folosite în acea situație.
  7. Aproximativ cât timp ai petrecut cu verificarea, incluzând adunarea informațiilor?
  8. Cât de ușor sau dificil a fost să obții informația de care aveai nevoie?
  9. Ce a funcționat bine sau te-a încurcat în acea situație?
    Dacă a existat o dificultate, poți spune și ce ai făcut mai departe sau ce consecință a avut. Dacă totul a mers bine, spune ce ți-a fost util.
  10. Ce aplicații, tabele sau alte metode ai încercat până acum pentru a-ți urmări banii?
    Menționează, dacă îți amintești, numele lor și de ce le-ai păstrat sau ai renunțat. Dacă folosești doar aplicația băncii ori nu urmărești cheltuielile separat, poți spune asta.
  11. Ai conectat vreodată un cont bancar la o aplicație din afara băncii, pentru ca ea să îți afișeze soldul sau tranzacțiile?
    Ne referim la accesul la datele contului, nu la adăugarea cardului într-un portofel digital sau la plata unei cumpărături.
  12. Dacă ai luat în calcul conectarea unui cont la o astfel de aplicație, ce a contat în decizia ta?
  13. În ultimele trei luni, ai avut vreo întrebare despre cheltuielile tale la care metodele folosite nu te-au ajutat să răspunzi?
    Dacă da, povestește pe scurt situația și ce ai încercat. Dacă nu, poți scrie «nu».
How counts, denominators and qualitative themes were calculated
  • Frozen comparison base: R001–R102, the first 102 submissions. IDs refer to submissions, not independently verified unique identities.
  • Recent checks: 83/102 (81.4%) report at least one attempt in 30 days. We exclude 16 “never” and 3 “do not remember” answers from recent-task measures, while retaining their comments as qualitative material.
  • Main behavioural subset: 73 respondents both used two or more banks/services and reported a recent attempt. A question about a total does not establish that all their accounts were included.
  • Time: self-reported intervals include information gathering for that check. They do not separately measure earlier setup or ongoing data entry. We do not turn these intervals into a precise average.
  • Methods: multiple selections can overlap. We count each full option once and do not split the answer at every comma or infer missing selections from later comments.
  • Themes: each submission is counted once per theme across free-text answers. Themes can overlap and include past experiences. A mention is not proof of a current unmet need.
  • Inconsistencies: R006 reports zero services but describes Revolut; R060 reports one but names two. We preserve the original choices. Comparison with the previous analysis found no quantitative changes in the first 102 rows; four “NA” text values had previously appeared as blanks.

Other findings: connections, unanswered questions and the extra response

External account connections: 85/102 (83.3%) have not tried; 6/102 (5.9%) currently use one; 9/102 (8.8%) used one previously; 2/102 (2.0%) stopped before completion. These groups do not form a churn cohort. Among the 73 recent multi-service checkers, just 4 currently connect accounts; two describe PFA/company accounting, so this cannot validate a personal open-banking workflow.

Unanswered spending questions: 66/102 (64.7%) explicitly answer “no”, 25/102 (24.5%) leave the final question blank, 1/102 (1.0%) supplies a dash and 10/102 (9.8%) describe a situation or wish. These ten include needs beyond SFA’s current scope. “No” also does not erase friction described elsewhere: R053 reports abandoning a laborious spreadsheet and still answers “no”.

Abandonment: six submissions explicitly describe stopping a method. Four mention effort or recording transactions. Other explanations include no perceived improvement in money left over and already staying within budget. Stopping one tool does not demonstrate demand for another.

Reading a total is different from keeping the data ready

“Îmi e mai dificil sa introduc datele în Excel, e că am multe itemuri dar după e ușor să-l consult”

R072 · survey answer · reported check: 2–5 minutes, fairly easy

The consultation is easy; entering the underlying data is the burden. The questionnaire does not isolate that maintenance time.

“Doar excel. Am un template mai complex si imi ofera tot ce vreau.”

R019 · survey answer

A personalised spreadsheet can already be the right solution. R100 also values the awareness gained by recording spending manually.

R084 reports checking a shared sheet in “2 seconds” once entries are kept consistent. R090 says cleaning imported internal transfers consumed almost as much time as manual entry. A fair comparison must include setup, collection, entry, corrections, reading and checking the answer, with one-time and recurring work reported separately.

Three behaviours, with different reasons to switch

Candidate segment

Tracking requires unwanted maintenance

Trigger: a periodic review, too much spent, or a question about subscriptions.

Workflow: check several apps, gather transactions and maintain a sheet or tracker. Personal categories and control are useful.

Observed consequence: R039 forgot entries; R053 found reconstruction too laborious and abandoned Excel; R072 struggles with entry.

Successful alternative

A personal system already works

Trigger: routine budget checks and deliberate financial control.

Workflow: an established sheet, template or app. Flexibility, shared access, history and discipline matter.

Observed result: R019 has everything needed; R084 checks a shared sheet quickly; R100 values manual attention.

Lower initial priority

The bank app or a simple rule is enough

Trigger: salary arrival, curiosity or checking available money.

Workflow: consult a balance or chart, pay obligations and allocate the remainder.

Observed result: R015 finds the function easily; R043 values existing categories. Other respondents do not need a detailed consolidated report.

05

Interviews

Five exploratory interviews are summarised below. They add context and counterexamples to the questionnaire; they are individual accounts, not population-level evidence. Participants have given permission for these recordings to be published.

A simple notes-based routine

28 October 2026 · 08:23

Current method and recent situation

The participant mainly uses one banking app and occasionally uses Revolut for individual purchases. Every few months, they review larger transactions over roughly three months, often after noticing a lower balance. Reconstructing spending from memory was difficult, so they check transaction history and note amounts on their phone.

What worked and what caused difficulty

Memory alone was insufficient, but the bank history provided the answer. A subscription charge briefly caused uncertainty about its renewal date; checking the payment details resolved it. The participant says the relevant information is only a few clicks away and is satisfied with a simple notes list rather than a detailed spreadsheet. Revolut purchases are infrequent and can be checked separately.

Evidence

  • 03:06–03:20: difficulty reconstructing past spending from memory.
  • 03:37–03:45: “verific istoricul și le menționez în notițe”; confirms using phone notes.
  • 05:12–06:18: uncertainty about subscription charges resolved through payment details.
  • 06:48–06:54: finding information in the banking history takes only a few clicks.

Effect on the hypothesis

Mixed. The interview supports a desire to understand past spending, but weakens the assumption that manual tracking is necessarily frustrating. There is no demonstrated major aggregation problem. Clearer visibility of recurring payments may be worth exploring while preserving the simplicity the participant values.

Understanding food spending

30 October 2026 · 05:13

Current method and recent situation

The participant recently started receiving employment income and has multiple accounts, but uses one account for the food spending discussed. After noticing increased food spending, they reviewed approximately three months of transactions, added the food expenses and calculated an average to understand consumption relative to salary.

What worked and what caused difficulty

For this one-account calculation, the participant says the current method is sufficient and reports no alternative method tried. They do not recall an unresolved question about where money had gone. They anticipate that checking several cards and adding the amounts could become inconvenient, but this was discussed as a possible future problem after an interviewer prompt, not as a recent multi-account difficulty.

Evidence

  • 00:21–00:51 and 01:24–01:49: reviews approximately three months of food spending and uses one account for those purchases.
  • 03:15–03:47: anticipates inconvenience from checking multiple accounts and manually adding amounts.
  • 03:55–03:59: “Pentru un singur card e ok de calculat.”

Effect on the hypothesis

Mixed. This is a concrete category-spending question, but the existing one-account method provides an adequate answer. The anticipated burden of account fragmentation remains a hypothesis to revisit if it occurs; this interview does not establish a present reason to switch to SFA.

Keeping track across two cards

31 October 2026 · 03:35

Current method and recent situation

The participant uses two bank cards, including Revolut, and reviews spending about monthly. They open each banking app, review transactions and keep notes. They sometimes forget spending on one card and then feel they have spent less than they actually have. In one example, they temporarily forgot an online gift purchase and had difficulty identifying the debit because the merchant name did not clearly match the shopping site.

What worked and what caused difficulty

The banking apps provide transaction records and the notes-based method is workable, though the participant says it is not the most efficient. Switching between cards makes spending harder to reconstruct, while unclear merchant names make individual purchases harder to recognise. The purchase was eventually remembered; no exact time to resolve it was reported.

Evidence

  • 00:48–01:08: forgetting spending on another card can create a misleading impression of staying within the intended amount.

Bringing spending together

1 November 2026 · 05:36

Current method and recent situation

The participant uses several payment cards and banking apps and has tried separate expense-tracking applications. To check monthly spending, they open each banking app’s transaction history and combine the information. They recall doing this to understand where their money had gone, but clarify that the example was not recent. Previous tracking apps required them to enter transactions, purchases and balances manually.

What worked and what caused difficulty

Banking histories provide transaction details, but getting a broader picture means switching between apps. Manual entry in tracking apps was described as annoying and time-consuming. Those apps supported categories, but the participant does not currently describe using a consolidated category or budget view. They expressed interest in automatic consolidation, broad categories, category budgets and alerts as spending approaches a limit or the next salary, these are stated preferences, not tested behaviours.

Evidence

  • 00:30–00:53: one banking app shows one card’s details, so other apps must be opened to combine spending information.
  • 02:31–02:53: “loghez manual fiecare tranzacție” — manual entry of transactions and the current balance is time-consuming.
  • 04:24–04:46: describes a desired app that combines transactions across cards and groups them into broad categories.
  • 04:55–05:22: requests category budgets and alerts about approaching a limit or managing spending until payday.

Effect on the hypothesis

Supported as an exploratory problem for a narrower segment. The participant describes using multiple apps to reconstruct spending and disliking the repeated manual entry required by tracking apps. It strengthens the case for testing a combined view without repeated entry with people who want one. The proposed categories and budget alerts followed feature-oriented questions, so they are weaker evidence than the participant’s account of past manual work. An observed comparison is still needed to establish whether SFA reduces effort and becomes useful in practice.

06

How discovery changes the product direction

The clearest next step is to investigate people who use at least two accounts for payments, already try to track spending, and can describe a recent, recurring problem with gathering or maintaining their information. Having multiple accounts alone is not enough to identify a need. This is a candidate segment, not a validated customer profile.

The questionnaire and five interviews help narrow what to test, but they do not yet tell us which feature will solve the problem. So rather than treating these as confirmed product decisions, we will use them to guide follow-up questions and same-task comparisons:

  1. Test whether a combined view reduces effort. The participant in Interview 03 describes forgetting purchases made on another card and difficulty recognising an unclear merchant name; the participant in Interview 05 describes combining information across banking apps and disliking manual entry in tracking apps. Both accounts support testing whether SFA can produce a useful, verifiable total with less effort. The Interview 05 participant’s interest in categories and budget alerts followed feature-oriented questions, so test these separately rather than treating them as established requirements. Do not assume that a general “health score” is useful.
  2. Keep Smart Tags as an open question. The survey contains one explicit tag request and two holiday-total examples; richer car-cost examples come from comments. These are useful prompts for follow-up, not enough evidence to build a full tagging system. Check whether people repeatedly need a specific breakdown that their current tools cannot provide.
  3. Compare statement imports before committing to open banking. 85/102 respondents had not tried connecting an account to an external app. That does not establish either demand or refusal. Test whether supported statement files can solve the target task first, and investigate bank coverage, trust and cost if exporting data proves to be a recurring obstacle.
  4. Look for repeated work before adding automation or exports. Some respondents describe successful personal spreadsheets and tracking methods, but we have not established how many repeatedly make the same corrections. Observe whether saved rules, batch edits or a spreadsheet export would remove effort without taking away useful control.
  5. Keep adjacent use cases exploratory. Four survey cases mention future obligations or deadlines, three describe shared or family tracking, and two explicitly mention a PFA or their own company. These small counts can guide follow-up questions, but do not yet justify expanding the product to household or business use.

These are questions and directions for the next test, not confirmed requirements or claims that the features have been implemented.

07

Are we ready for Customer Validation?

Yes for focused customer validation. Not yet for a repeatable sales test.

Validation 1 outcome: Partially validated, with a clearer target segment

Our exploratory research provides evidence that multi-service financial management is a relevant context and that a subset of users experiences friction when consolidating financial information.

Across 102 questionnaire responses:

  • 88/102 respondents reported using at least two financial services.
  • 73/88 multi-service respondents confirmed attempting to check their spending within the previous 30 days.
  • 33/73 recent multi-service checkers reported spending at least six minutes on the task.
  • 15/102 respondents described manual effort, missing information or difficulties maintaining consistent tracking.
  • 51/73 recent multi-service checkers considered their task easy, demonstrating that existing solutions already work for many users.

These are exploratory findings from our sample, not population-level estimates.

What we learned

Our strongest opportunity is not simply displaying multiple bank accounts in one place. It is reducing unnecessary effort and helping users obtain accurate, actionable answers about their finances.

The next validation phase will focus on participants who currently experience friction, while also including users who are satisfied with their existing solutions as counterexamples.

Conclusion: Validation 1 provides sufficient exploratory evidence to proceed to focused solution testing. It does not yet establish product-market fit or commercial viability.
